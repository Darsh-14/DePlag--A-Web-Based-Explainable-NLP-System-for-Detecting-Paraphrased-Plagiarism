Abstract
In today’s world, Paraphrased plagiarism unfolds as a quiet yet an alarming cri-
sis for academic domain. It is different from the simple copy-paste plagiarism.
In this minute form of plagiarism, structural or word-for-word replacements are
made using semantically equivalent words or phrases. This changes the con-
tent but keeps the original stolen idea intact. Traditional detection systems,
depend heavily on keyword-based overlap, n-gram matching, or digital finger-
printing. They can efficiently point out copy-paste plagiarism but fail to catch
complex paraphrased transformations. We have seen recent advancements in
Natural Language Processing (NLP), like transformer-based architectures and
sentence embedding models that have significantly improved our ability to detect
semantic similarity. But, most of the current implementations are ”black-box”
systems. They produce similarity scores and binary conclusions but do not pro-
vide any human-interpretable logic regarding why a text was flagged or which
specific linguistic shifts or structural changes led to the decision.This poses a
serious issue especially in education, where accountability and corrective feed-
back are must needed. A raw vague percentage data is not enough to flag a
work as plagiarized, instructors and students need to have a detailed under-
standing of the nature and paraphrasing strategies that exist and were used in
the content. There are existing Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as atten-
tion visualizations, but they fail to provide meaningful insights because they
are not extended till explicit linguistic reasoning and function at a surface level.
Also, these systems lack the efficiency to provide counterfactual logic, which is
essential for understanding how minor structural adjustments can influence a
model’s judgment. To fill this gap, and help the world deal with the plagia-
rism issues in a better way, this paper introduces a hybrid, explainable, and
counterfactual NLP-based framework for detecting paraphrased plagiarism. Our
approach is based on a combination of core concepts: transformer-driven seman-
tic modeling, based on linguistic paraphrase typologies, and the generation of
1
actionable counterfactual explanations. Our system aligns content at the sen-
tence level and deconstruct specific operations—such as synonym substitution,
syntactic restructuring, and polarity reversal, thus providing a roadmap of the
author’s intent. We have materialized this framework into a web-based proto-
type, DePlag, which shifts the focus from purely corrective model towards a
transparent, reasoning-based system. By introducing the combined features of
high-level semantic awareness with counterfactual logic, this work advances the
development of trustworthy and scholarly constructive tools for the academic
community.Key Technical Highlights Included in this Draft are :The SBERT

1 Introduction
The space of academic integrity is currently under tremendous change due to the
rapid commercialization of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Generative AI (GenA-
I). Earlier, plagiarism existed only with simple word-for-word overlap but in today’s
world it is a complex phase dominated by semantic obfuscation and automated text
generation.
Tang et al. (1) recently pointed out a growing problem, it’s getting incredibly
difficult to tell the difference between what a person wrote and what an AI produced.
This makes plagiarism detection more complex as the traditional systems we used
to rely on, like looking for specific writing styles or simple word-matching patterns
don’t promise accuracy anymore. This disruption is not just technological. Yusuf et
al. (2) describe Gen-AI to have equal pros and cons. While it offers students with
a personalized learning experience, it simultaneously poses an existential threat to
integrity as it defies them from thinking and binds them to a loop of prompting. This
sentiment is resonated by Basyoni et al. (10), who warn that as education migrates
toward the Metaverse future, academic content is no longer written by human alone, it
is a mix of human and AI interpretations, letting AI hold an extensive role in shaping
the contents.
In a systematic review of higher education integrity, Bittle and El-Gayar (3) iden-
tify that students are utilizing Gen-AI to generate complex texts that help them
bypass traditional plagiarism detection tools. Today, we face Adversarial Paraphrasing.
Perkins et al. (4) demonstrated that students can bypass leading detectors, includ-
ing industry standards like Turnitin—by using recursive paraphrasing strategies such
as synonym replacement and back translation. These techniques effectively lower the
2
variation and predictability patterns that previous detectors rely upon. This drawback
was further exposed by recent evaluations of tools like ChatGPT (5).
Research Gap The inadequacy of current tools is well-documented. Weber-Wulff
et al. (6) evaluated and found that detector systems are prone to high False Negative
rates when facing obfuscated text that are cleverly disguised, and high False Positive
rates by flagging the non-native English work as plagiarized. This makes people not
trust such systems anymore. Nelson et al. (9) and Karkoulian et al. (8) conducted
a research on how students actually feel about this. Students accepted that it is not
ethical to use GEN-AI, but they justified their actions by pointing out that the schools
lacked transparent ways to check their work. Basically, they feel that if the rules and
the tools are a mystery, it’s hard to follow them. Lancaster (7) says that without
proper proof, we cannot accuse someone for plagiarism as this often backfires, creating
a conflict between institutional policy and student practice.
Proposed Solution The threat exists far beyond the undergraduate classroom.
Guba and Tsivinskaya (11) warn that experts are being pressurized to publish more
papers which leads them to use GenAI to mask works of others, thus innovation not
being original anymore. Campino (65) describes this as ”AI-assisted ghostwriting.”
Even sensitive fields are vulnerable; Erol et al. (12) and Ibrahim (13) caution that
medical and legal education face risks as AI is smart enough to generate believable but
factually incorrect case studies. The urgency for a solution is underscored by Awasthi
et al. (14) and Eshet (15) says that students are well aware of the plagiarism policies
but they continue to exploit it, due to the detection gap that still existed.
To overcome these limitations, our paper proposes DePlag– A Hybrid, Explainable
Plagiarism Detection System (HEPDS). As we know, we can not rely solely on opaque
”Black Box” traditional models. That would be insufficient. So, we introduce a system
that combines the semantic depth of Sentence-BERT (SBERT) with the computational
efficiency of lexical alignment, enhanced by a novel Explainable AI (XAI) visualization
layer with counterfactual reasoning.
2 Related Work
The evolution of plagiarism detection can be divided into three different eras: (1)
Traditional Lexical Detection, (2) Deep Learning & Stylometry, and (3) The Modern
Era of Hybrid & Explainable Architectures.
Lexical & Statistical Approaches Early research in plagiarism detection
was based on surface-level similarity. A major landmark survey was conducted, in
which Folt´ynek et al. (19) divided these methods into character-based (n-gram) and
vector-based approaches. Samen (24) refined n-gram analysis for scholarly documents
specifically using metadata to determine how closely two documents are related. It
was noted that 3-gram overlaps were good to detect verbatim copying, but they fail
against ”paraphrased plagiarism” where words are substituted but meaning remains
same. A solution wss proposed by El-Rashidy et al. which said that Support Vec-
tor Machines (SVM) could be used and more effective results could be obtained from
the available methods(20). To detect GEN-AI content, these attempts were not much
effective as they lacked the semantic understanding required. Similarly, Kamat et al.
3
(25) came up with the idea of using supervised classifiers to detect both the exact
and the paraphrased document. Although, this method brought some success but a
significant degradation was noticed in the performance in case of shuffled content.
Plagiarism Detection in Other Domains
Over the years, plagiarism detection has moved beyond simple text analysis. It
includes complex systems theory and even code analysis now. De Santis et al. (71) talks
about using of Complex Systems theory to find out the differences between human-
written text and LLM-generated text rather than depending on the traditional sta-
tistical patterns. In this research, he analyzed whether the patterns used by Natural
Language Generation(NLG) are enough to replicate the hierarchial structures seen in
human intelligence. In the domain of software engineering, Zhang and Saber (23) and
Romanov et al. (61) addresses plagiarism and AI generated content and proposes an
integrated technique to verify the authorship of both natural language and program-
ming source code. Ito et al. (72) and Suljic and Hossain (73) reveal about the use
of neural machine translation models. We structure or train such models to detect
similarity and authorship of binary codes. Furthermore, Shen and Lin (60) made a
hybrid model for detecting plagiarism in patent claims known as ’PatentGrapher’. All
these cases together tell us how versatile it is to use graph neural networks in other
specialized domains.
Development of Hybrid Systems It was soon realized that it is difficult for
a single model to handle all the operations. So, the concept of Hybrid Architectures
was born. Jieren et al. (30) proposed a system that worked in two stages: It filters
candidates using lightweight metrics before applying heavy Transformer classification.
We use this same logic in our work so as to ensure scalability. Similarly, Tang et al. (29)
utilized FAISS combined with sentence embeddings to handle large-scale retrieval. Setu
et al. (31) and Hassan et al. (33) further enhanced Semantic Textual Similarity(STS)
using web scraping and chaotic perturbation optimization techniques.
Explainable mechanism At present, literature does not have a an effective
feature to explain the plagiarism detected. As Parvathy et al. (74) says automated
assessment systems must provide constructive feedback to be academically effective;
a simple plagiarism percentage score is vague rather than informative. Manzoor et al.
(76) explored more and analyzed how to identify plagiarism by examining writing style,
lexical diversity, and syntax changes within a single document and decoded intrinsic
detection metrics to differentiate between malicious intent and harmless errors. Luo et
al. (41) and Najjar et al. (42) emphasize on the necessity of ”Local Interpretations” to
achieve this goal. Techniques such as LIME (48) and SHAP (49) have been adapted
by Schulte et al. (44) and Cesarini et al. (43) to provide feature-level importance
scores. Nasim et al. (45) proposed a framework for Trustworthy XAI. He suggested
that visually highlighting suspicious text spans improves user acceptance. This was
further supported by Vilone and Longo (46).
Adversarial Threats The robust and advanced system should be capable enough
to detect adversarial plagiarism. Macko et al. (50) and Maiti et al. (52) studied author-
ship obfuscation and showed that automated paraphrasing tools can strongly reduce
detection performance. There are problems created by Gen-AI data enhancement. Li et
al. (54) and Stefanovi´c et al. Stefanovi´c et al. (55) mention these problems for reliable
4
and accurate detection. Alharthi et al. (64) and Sharmila et al.(75) proposed respon-
sible mitigation methods using fuzzy logic and ensemble learning (62; 67) to address
these issues. Beyond authorship detection, applied these similarity-based methods were
applied to fake news and tweet detection by Bezerra et al. (66) and Noronha (63).
Thus, we get to know about the board applications and adaptability of transformer-
based systems (68; 69).
3 Problem Definition
We have moved beyond simple word-matching techniques. Today, deep neural networks
like BERT are used to detect plagiarism even when a sentence has been carefully
rewritten or when words are replaced with synonyms. However the major problem still
remains .These systems can tell us that a document is highly likely to be a plagiarized
,but they do not clearly explain why or point to the exact parts of the text that caused
this decision.
This creates a serious problem in schools and universities. A student cannot be
accused of academic dishonesty just because a trained system gives a high probability
score . In an academic environment, we need more than a number; we need clear proof.
It should be possible to show a student exactly where their logic appears to be copied
and explain why.
Let Dsusp denote a suspicious document (query) and Dsrc denote a source docu-
ment from the reference corpus C. Following the granular approach recommended by
Zhou et al. (2025) suggested , each document is treated as an ordered sequence of
sentences S = {s1, s2, ..., sn}.
The main challenge in plagiarism detection is to define a mapping function f :
Ssusp →Ssrc so that a sentence si ∈Dsusp is aligned with a sentence sj ∈Dsrc when
both express the same meaning , even if the wording and structure are different .
We define two types of similarity that are considered:
• Lexical Similarity (L): It is omputed using the Jaccard Coefficient over sets of
n-grams and , measures surface-level word overlap.
• Semantic Similarity (S): It is Computed using Cosine Similarity between dense
vector embeddings ei and ej generated by the SBERT Bi-Encoder.
Adversarial Paraphrased Plagiarism refer to situations where a text keeps the
same meaning as the original source but it intentionally changed in wording to reduce
direct word overlap (e.g., by replacing words with synonyms or changing the sentence
structure).
Accordingly, a sentence pair (si, sj) is classified as Paraphrased Plagiarism if it
satisfies the dual condition:
P(si, sj) ⇐⇒(L(si, sj) < τlex) ∧(S(si, sj) ≥τsem)

Where:
• τlex is the lexical threshold (e.g., 0.3) below which traditional n-gram detectors fail.
• τsem is the semantic threshold (e.g., 0.75) indicating high conceptual similarity.

3.1 Explainability Requirement:
A simple binary detection result, expressed as F ∈{0, 1}, is usually not enough for a
teacher who needs to discuss to a student about their work. When a system only simply
labels a paper as ”plagiarized” without any explaination, it does not support learning
or improvement. For this reason ,we believe that a user need a local explanation E
that clearly shows why a particular text segment was flagged. To provide this clarity,
we adopt the taxonomy of Local Interpretations defined by Luo et al. We define
the explanation function as a token-level attribution map. This allows the system to
exactly which words or phrases contributed most to the detection result. By converting
high-dimensional model data into a visual representation, the system moves away from
a purely punitive model toward a transparent and understandable one. This ensures
that before anyone is accused of misconduct a clear evidence is available, instead of
any unclear statistical scores.

4 Proposed Explainable Framework
Traditional ”Black Box” neural detectors have limits , so our framework takes a differ-
ent approach . Instead of just giving an end-to-end prediction , it combines three clear
steps : (1) Hybrid Semantic-Lexical Alignment, (2) Automated Obfuscation Forensics,
and (3) Visual Counterfactual Explanation.
The first step identifies potential suspicious sentence pairs. We use the SBERT Bi-
Encoder to convert sentences into 768-dimensional vectors. Unlike old n-gram methods
(Samen), which miss the meaning behind words, SBERT captures the actual intent of
the text.

Once a pair is aligned, the system must identify how the text was obfuscated.
Drawing on the taxonomy of ”Authorship Obfuscation” defined by Macko et al. we
employ a linguistic typology approach. Both sentences are parsed using dependency
parsers (via spaCy) to generate syntactic trees. We compare the graph structures of the
source (Tsrc) and target (Tsusp) sentences and identify specific obfuscation strategies:
• Synonym Substitution: It is detected when the syntactic structure (edges and depth)
remains constant (Tsrc ∼= Tsusp), but leaf nodes differ but share a WordNet. This
opposes the Simple Paraphrasing plagiarism.
• Syntactic Obfuscation: It is detected by analyzing dependency shifts. Specifically,
we flag a Voice Change if the object (dobj) in Tsrc becomes the subject (nsubjpass)
in Tsusp, followed by inserting of auxiliary verbs. This explicitly detects the Active-
to-Passive rewriting strategy.
8
• Structural Restructuring: It is detected when the tree depth or branching factor
changes significantly (∆depth > 2) while the semantic embedding vector remains
stable.
The innovation in the HEPDS architecture is its transparency in comparison to
binary classification. We introduce a Glass-Box Transparency Layer that decomposes
the plagiarism decision into granular linguistic evidence. This is achieved through
two coupled mechanisms: Gradient-Based Feature Attribution and Perturbation-Based
Counterfactual Generation.

Analytical Significance Most generic explainer tools function around local
boundary conditions, but GWIE uses the differentiable nature of the SBERT embed-
ding space. By effectively removing tokens with the highest gradient magnitude ϕ, the
algorithm effectively identifies the high impact words of the paraphrase, usually the
specific synonyms or verbs substituted by the student to evade N-gram detection. The
resulting set C serves as irrefutable proof of the paraphrase attempt. As this trans-
former works analyzes work in two layers, it transforms the output from a punishing
into a formative learning tool. As proposed by Parvathy et al. [65], providing the
”Minimal Reversal Set” C allows the student to visualize the boundary between legit-
imate synthesis and patch-writing. By explicitly flagging the Active-to-Passive shift or
the Synonym Swapping patterns, the system tells the user the specific failures of their
writing process, resulting in long-term academic integrity.

5.1 Hyperparameter Optimization and Sensitivity Analysis
The performance of the Hybrid SBERT framework depends strongly on how well the
two decision thresholds are set : the semantic lower-bound α and the lexical upper-
bound β. If these values are not chosen carefully , then system may either produce
to high False Acceptance Rates (flagging coincidentally similar text) or high False
Rejection Rates (missing subtle paraphrasing).
To identify the most effective parameter values, the calibration process was for-
mulated as an optimization task over the F1-score surface, allowing us to select the
threshold combination that provides the best balance between precision and recall.
We performed a rigorous grid search over the parameter space α ∈[0.60, 0.95] and
β ∈[0.20, 0.60] using the PAWS adversarial dataset. The improvement objective is
given as:

6 System Architecture
The proposed HEPDS framework operates as a multi-stage pipeline designed to sep-
arate semantic understanding from lexical verification. As illustrated in Figure 6, the
system processes input text through parallel analysis streams before giving a final
explainable decision.
The architecture is composed of three primary stages:
15
Fig. 6: Workflow of the Explainable plagiarism detection system. It shows the archi-
tecture from document input to counterfactual reasoning output.
Preprocessing
At first, a suspicious target document (Dsusp) and a candidate reference corpus are
input in the pipeline. The noise data is reduced by the Preprocessing Module. Standard
16
normalization techniques using the NLTK library are used like tokenization, stop-
word removal, and lemmatization are used. This standardized input is then divided
into two parallel processing streams. To capture both verbatim copying and subtle
paraphrasing, the system uses a hybrid extraction strategy:
• The Lexical Stream path focuses on surface-level similarity. We use N-Gram layer
and break the text into unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams. Then we use the Jaccard
Similarity coefficient L). This gives the exact overlap between the suspicious target
text and the source. This stream is highly effective at detecting Cut-and-Paste
plagiarism.
• The Semantic Stream path focuses on contextual meaning. We utilize the SBERT Bi-
Encoder (specifically sentence-t5-base) to project sentences into a 768-dimensional
dense vector space. A Cosine Similarity calculation (S) is then performed between
the embedding vectors. This stream is effective against synonym replacement and
sentence reordering.
The core innovation lies in the Dual-Threshold Logic Gate. The system applies
conditional thresholds (α for semantic, β for lexical) to classify the text content.
• If the logic gate flags a sentence as Suspicious, it is passed to the Forensics Module.
This module performs tasks such as detecting Active-to-Passive voice inversions or
synonym density to confirm the nature of the obfuscation.
• In the end the XAI Engine generates a ”Glass-Box” report (E), visualizing the
specific evidence (Heatmaps and Counterfactuals) to provide the use with solid proof
of plagiarism.
Implementation and Development
The HEPDS framework relies on a decoupled architecture. It separates the compu-
tationally intensive model training pipeline from the lightweight, real-time inference
microservice. The system was developed using Python 3.9+, and using the ecosystem
detailed below.
We have the training module that is responsible for learning the decision boundary
between original and plagiarized text. This ensures consistency and balance.
• We used Pandas to manage the training corpus. We created a balanced dataset
using samples of 15,000 sentence pairs. This prevented any imbalance bias in class.
This helps the model to respond equally to positive and negative cases.
• It does not depend on pre-built metric libraries. Instead we executed custom vector
based operations using NumPy.
– Embeddings were generated using the sentence-t5-base transformer. We
computed Cosine similarity using manual linear algebra that is dot product
normalized by Euclidean norms to ensure precise control over floating-point
stability.
– A custom Jaccard overlap function was implemented using Python’s native set
operations for speed. +
17
• The final decision logic is a Logistic Regression classifier from Scikit-learn. It was
configured with class weight=’balanced’. This specific hyperparameter ensures
that the model avoids false negatives more efficiently.
Inference Engine and Deployment: The runtime application is served using a Flask
microservice, designed to separate the inference logic from the user interface.
• The heavy T5 transformer and spaCy NER pipelines are not initialized per request,
they are pre-loaded into global memory during the application startup. This is done
to minimize the latency.
• We integrated spaCy (en core web sm) specifically for Named Entity Recognition
(NER) that allows the system to identify shared proper nouns (e.g., ”Algorithm
X”) independent of the surrounding sentence structure.
• The trained classifier and logic gates are arranged serially using Pickle. This allows
a rapid deployment and versioning of the decision logic so that the underlying
transformer does not require any retraining.

7 Datasets
To evaluate the performance of the proposed HEPDS framework, we constructed a
composite dataset that reflects a wide range of academic misconduct. This dataset
includes everything from simple “copy–paste” plagiarism to more complex, adversarial
paraphrasing produced by Large Language Models (LLMs). The data were chosen to
ensure both diversity and difficulty, so that the system is properly stress-tested against
the different paraphrasing strategies discussed in earlier sections.
The semantic analysis module is built on well-established benchmark datasets that
measure a model’s ability to recognize preserved meaning despite changes in wording
and structure. Microsoft Research Paraphrase Corpus (MSRP) was used as the pri-
mary baseline dataset for semantic training, as it provides reliable sentence pairs for
learning paraphrase-level semantic similarity . This corpus is widely regarded as the
goldmine for paraphrase detection. It consists of 5,801 sentence pairs extracted from
news sources over an 18-month period. MSRP is not like any other synthetic dataset,
it captures the natural variance of human writing. It presents a significant challenge
because the ”paraphrased” pairs often share very few n-grams, forcing our SBERT
model to rely on deep semantic vectors rather than surface-level matching. We stick to
the standard split of 4,076 training pairs and 1,725 test pairs. This allows our results
to be directly comparable to state-of-the-art models like those reviewed by Zhou et al.
To prevent our system from falling into any unnecessary words matching loop, we
incorporated the PAWS (Paraphrase Adversaries from Word Scrambling) dataset.
• PAWS is specifically designed to fool simple neural networks. It contains sentence
pairs that share high lexical overlap (e.g., the same words arranged differently) but
have completely different meanings.
18
• Consider the difference between ”Flights from New York to Florida” and ”Flights
from Florida to New York.” A standard Jaccard similarity check would score this
as a 100% match, potentially flagging a false positive. By training on PAWS, our
Hybrid Logic learns to distinguish between genuine plagiarism and mere keyword
coincidence, a critical distinction emphasized by Vahtola et al.
These datasets were still not enough. While paraphrase datasets test semantic
understanding, we still required complex datasets with intentional obfuscation to
detect academic cheating.
We used subsets from the PAN Plagiarism Corpus (PAN-PC-11), which simulates
the real-world conditions of academic submissions. This dataset is unique because it
introduces varying levels of ”obfuscation,” classified into:
• None (Verbatim): Exact copying, easily caught by any Lexical Stream (Scorelex).
• Random Text Operations: Manual insertion, deletion, or shuffling of words.
• Translation: Text that has been translated from German/Spanish to English and
back, a technique often used by students to scrub digital fingerprints.
We tested our Explainability (XAI) module against these diverse attack vectors to
ensure that our Explainability (XAI) module can correctly identify and visualize the
specific type of transformation being used.
To address the limitations of the previous collections AI- generated text is included
in this study. We followed the methodology of Tang et al. and Macko et al. and
constructed a custom validation set comprising 1,000 abstract-length paragraphs.
• 50% of the text was human-written (source: arXiv papers) , while we generated the
remaining using AI like GPT-4 and Claude 3. The prompt was given as ”rewrite
this academic text to avoid plagiarism detection”.
• In this subset we specifically focus on the ”hallucination” and ”smoothness” features
of modern LLMs. It helps us to evaluate whether our SBERT embeddings can go
beyond the polished surface of AI-generated text and find the underlying source
material. This would be a bridge to the detection gap mentioned by Perkins et al.
before.
We focused on the preprocessing rules first, then fed the data into the HEPDS
pipeline to ensure clarity.
• We changed all the text to lowercase, stripped the special characters, but kept the
punctuation intact where necessary for syntactic parsing.
• Long documents were segmented into sentence-level units using NLTK, as our
SBERT model is optimized for short-to-medium text spans.
• We meticulously removed metadata, citations, and mathematical formulas to pre-
vent the model from overfitting on non-textual objects, ensuring that the detection
is based solely on linguistic content.
Performance is evaluated on two axes: Detection and Explainability. We report
Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Given the class imbalance in plagiarism
detection that is most of the text is original, F1-score is the primary metric. We
measure Reasoning and Counterfactual Stability (are the suggested edits consistent?).
19
We also employ human evaluation scores, where domain experts rate the usefulness of
the generated explanations on a Likert scale.
8 Discussion
Switching from ”Black-Box” detection to the explainable HEPDS framework offers
notable educational benefits, although it brings about certain computational compro-
mises. A major methodological issue in our Hybrid approach is the delay caused by
the XAI Engine.
• Latency Analysis: Standard cosine similarity checks (used in the Semantic Stream)
operate in O(1) time after vector indexing. However, the generation of counterfactual
explanations requires iterative gradient queries, which increases inference time by
approximately 40% compared to baseline BERT models.
• Justification: While this overhead is non-negligible, it is justifiable for academic
integrity use cases where **accuracy and transparency** outweigh millisecond-level
speed. Future work will focus on hierarchical indexing (FAISS) to prune the search
space before triggering the expensive Forensic Module.
As noted by Parvathy et al. automated assessment systems must provide construc-
tive feedback to be truly effective. The HEPDS framework shifts the paradigm from
purely punitive detection to educational support.
• Distinguishing Intent: By visualizing the specific linguistic transformations (e.g.,
Active-to-Passive inversion), the system helps instructors distinguish between mali-
cious ”Contract Cheating” and benign ”Patchwriting” (poor paraphrasing due to
lack of skill).
• Student Feedback: The ”Glass-Box” report acts as a formative tool, showing stu-
dents exactly how their writing lacks originality, thereby encouraging better citation
practices rather than just avoiding a similarity score.
A critical limitation of current semantic models is the potential for ”hallucinat-
ing” similarity in AI-generated text. As discussed by De Santis et al., Large Language
Models (LLMs) often produce text with low perplexity and specific statistical
watermarks.
• While our Semantic Stream detects paraphrased content effectively, it may struggle
to differentiate between a student’s original thought and an AI’s synthetic output
if the semantic vectors align perfectly.
• Future iterations of HEPDS will incorporate intrinsic detection metrics (Burstiness
and Perplexity) as surveyed by Manzoor et al. By adding a ”Synthetic Text Detec-
tor” stream alongside the existing Lexical and Semantic streams, the architecture
can be extended to flag AI-hallucinated content that mimics academic style.

9 Conclusion
In this study, we have tackled the growing ”arms race” in academic honesty by
introducing a Hybrid, Explainable Plagiarism Detection System (HEPDS). As the dis-
tinction between text written by humans and that generated by machines becomes
increasingly unclear situation Tang et al. refer to as a ”scientific crisis”conventional
detection techniques have found it difficult to keep up. Our findings show that depend-
ing only on unclear probability scores is no longer enough. Rather, we need to shift
towards ”Glass-Box” systems that not only identify similarities but also clarify the
origin of those similarities.
The framework introduced here connects the accuracy of deep learning with the
clarity needed for educational purposes. By separating semantic understanding (using
SBERT) from lexical checking (using Jaccard), our hybrid approach effectively detects
”Adversarial Paraphrasing,” which often bypasses basic n-gram detectors, as noted by
Samen. Importantly, the inclusion of a Forensic Linguistic Module enables the system
to differentiate between intentional attempts to obscure content (such as synonym
replacement attacks) and harmless edits, resolving the false-positive issues highlighted
by Weber-Wulff et al.
22
Beyond the technical framework, this study promotes a change in teaching meth-
ods. As Parvathy et al. argue, evaluation tools should be supportive rather than merely
disciplinary. By offering counterfactual explanations demonstrating precisely how a
student’s sentence structure resembles a source HEPDS shifts plagiarism detection
from a ”monitoring” function to a resource for improving academic writing. This open-
ness is crucial for bridging the ”trust gap” identified by Nelson et al., helping students
recognize the distinction between acceptable influence and academic misconduct.
Although our early findings are encouraging, the ”Generative Metaverse” outlined
by Basyoni et al. brings ongoing challenges. Our current model mainly focuses on
text-to-text paraphrasing; however, the growth of multimodal AI (such as image-to-
text generation) remains an unexplored area. Future developments of this research
will concentrate on three main aspects:
• Scalability: optimizing the counterfactual search algorithms using hierarchical
indexing (FAISS) to handle university-scale repositories in real-time.
• GenAI Resilience: integrating intrinsic metrics (such as Burstiness and Perplexity)
to better flag ”hallucinated” plagiarism from LLMs like GPT-4.
• Empirical Validation: conducting large-scale user studies with educators and
students to quantify the impact of explainable feedback on learning outcomes.
Ultimately, we believe that the future of academic integrity lies not in stricter
surveillance, but in smarter, more transparent tools that empower students to take
ownership of their own originality.
